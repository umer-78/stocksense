The formulas

These are the same formulas the StockSense app uses for every variant, so you can check any number it shows you.

Average daily sales

daily sales = units sold ÷ calendar days in the period

Count every calendar day, including days with zero sales. Averaging only over days that had sales overstates demand.

Days to stockout

days to stockout = units on hand ÷ daily sales

Lead-time demand and safety stock

lead-time demand = daily sales × lead time (days) safety stock = lead-time demand × safety % (round up)

Lead-time demand is what you expect to sell while waiting for a delivery. Safety stock covers a late shipment or a busier-than-usual week.

Reorder point

reorder point = lead-time demand + safety stock (round up)

When stock on hand falls to the reorder point, place the order. Waiting longer means the new stock arrives after you have sold out.

How many units to order

order quantity = daily sales × target days + safety stock − on hand

If the result is zero or negative you have enough stock; no order is needed yet.

Worked example

A running shoe sold 42 pairs in the last 60 days and you have 6 left. Your supplier takes 14 days, you keep a 20% buffer, and each order should last 30 days.

  1. Daily sales: 42 ÷ 60 = 0.70 per day.
  2. Days to stockout: 6 ÷ 0.70 = 8.6 days.
  3. Lead-time demand: 0.70 × 14 = 9.8 units.
  4. Safety stock: 9.8 × 20% = 1.96, rounded up to 2 units.
  5. Reorder point: 9.8 + 2 = 11.8, rounded up to 12 units. You have 6, so you are past it.
  6. Order quantity: 0.70 × 30 = 21, plus 2 safety stock = 23, minus 6 on hand = 17 units.

Stock runs out in 8.6 days but a new delivery takes 14, so this product will sell out for about five days even if you order today. That is the case the calculator marks critical.

Choosing your inputs

  • Sales period: 60 days suits most products. Use a shorter window for fast-changing trends and a longer one for slow sellers. With fewer than 14 days of history, treat the result as a rough guess.
  • Lead time: use what actually happened on your last few orders, not the supplier's quoted time. Include shipping and the time to get stock on the shelf.
  • Safety stock: 20% is a sensible start. Go higher for unreliable suppliers, long lead times or volatile sales.
  • Target cover: match how often you place orders. Reordering monthly suggests about 30 days.

Common mistakes

  • Counting days you were sold out as slow days. If a product was out of stock for part of the period, its true demand was higher than the sales show. Shorten the period to when it was in stock.
  • Ignoring lead time. Reordering when you hit zero guarantees a gap. The reorder point exists to account for the wait.
  • One setting for every product. A supplier overseas and one down the road need different lead times.

Questions

What is the reorder point formula?

Reorder point = lead-time demand + safety stock, where lead-time demand is average daily sales multiplied by supplier lead time in days.

How do I calculate safety stock?

A simple, common approach is a percentage of lead-time demand, such as 20%. Raise it for unreliable suppliers or volatile sales, lower it for steady sellers with dependable delivery.
